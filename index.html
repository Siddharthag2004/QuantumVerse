Quantum Computing Timeline

From Einstein's thought experiments to today's quantum processors

1935
EPR Paradox

Einstein, Podolsky & Rosen publish the EPR paradox, questioning quantum mechanics' completeness. Seeds of entanglement.

1964
Bell's Theorem

John Bell proves quantum mechanics can't be explained by local hidden variables. Entanglement is real.

1982
Feynman's Vision

Richard Feynman proposes quantum computers to simulate quantum systems — classical computers can't do it efficiently.

1994
Shor's Algorithm

Peter Shor discovers quantum factoring — threatens RSA encryption. Launches the quantum computing race.

1996
Grover's Algorithm

Lov Grover finds O(√N) quantum search. First practical quantum speedup for everyday problems.

2019
Quantum Supremacy

Google's Sycamore processor performs a specific task in 200 seconds that would take classical supercomputers ~10,000 years.

2023
Error Correction Milestones

IBM, Google demonstrate logical qubits with below-threshold error rates. The path to fault tolerance becomes clear.

2026+
NISQ Advantage Era

VQE, QAOA algorithms showing practical advantages in chemistry and optimization. Post-quantum cryptography standardized by NIST.

Quantum Hardware Gallery

Real physical architectures driving the quantum revolution

Superconducting Processors

Qubits constructed from micro-fabricated superconducting circuits, cooled down to near-absolute zero (10 millikelvin) inside a dilution refrigerator to minimize decoherence.

Superconducting Circuits

Trapped Ion Devices

Charged atoms suspended in electromagnetic fields in a vacuum, manipulated using precision laser beams. Known for high gate fidelities and long coherence times.

Electromagnetic Ion Trapping

Photonic Quantum Computers

Utilizes single photons of light routed through silicon waveguides to perform computing steps. Functions perfectly at room temperature without cryogenics.

Silicon Photonics

Neutral Atom Processors

Individual neutral rubidium atoms suspended in arrays by focused laser beams (optical tweezers). Highly scalable and capable of simulating complex quantum systems.

Optical Tweezers

Topological Computers

Qubits protected by braiding Majorana fermions in nanostructures. Designed to be intrinsically immune to noise, removing the need for massive error correction.

Majorana Fermions

Silicon Spin Qubits

Utilizes the spin of individual electrons trapped inside silicon quantum dots. Highly promising because they leverage standard semiconductor manufacturing.

Silicon Quantum Dots
